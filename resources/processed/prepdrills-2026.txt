READ AN ACADEMIC PASSAGE
Urban Heat Islands
Anyone who has walked through a city on a hot summer day knows that urban areas can feel
considerably warmer than the countryside nearby. Scientists call this effect the “urban heat island.”
On average, the temperature in a large city can be several degrees higher than in the surrounding
rural areas, and the difference is often greatest at night.
There are several reasons for this. Materials such as concrete and asphalt absorb heat from the sun
during the day and release it slowly after dark. Tall buildings trap this warm air in narrow streets, while
a lack of trees means there is little shade and less cooling from the moisture that plants release.
Cars, air conditioners, and factories add still more heat.
Urban heat islands are more than a matter of comfort. Higher temperatures increase the demand for
electricity, worsen air pollution, and can be dangerous for the elderly and the sick. Fortunately, cities
can take action. Planting trees, creating parks, and painting roofs white to reflect sunlight have all
been shown to lower urban temperatures.

READ AN ACADEMIC PASSAGE
The Bilingual Brain
For much of the twentieth century, many educators believed that raising a child with two languages
was harmful. They worried that the child would confuse the two systems and master neither. Today,
research has overturned this view. Studies show that children who grow up bilingual reach the same
language milestones as other children, and in some ways their thinking is sharper.
One reason is that a bilingual person's brain is constantly deciding which language to use and
suppressing the other. This mental exercise appears to strengthen what psychologists call “executive
function”—the ability to focus attention, ignore distractions, and switch between tasks. Some
researchers have even found that lifelong bilingualism may delay the onset of certain age-related
memory problems by several years.

Listen to a talk on a podcast about studying, then answer questions 15–18.
Podcast Host: Have you ever crammed all night for a test, only to forget everything a week later? If so, you've
run into one of the most reliable findings in psychology: the spacing effect. Simply put, we remember
information far better when we study it in short sessions spread out over time, rather than in one long,
exhausting block.
Why does this happen? Each time you return to material after a gap, your brain has to work a little harder to
recall it, and that extra effort strengthens the memory. Cramming, by contrast, feels productive because the
information is fresh in the moment, but it fades quickly once the test is over.
The practical lesson is clear. If you have five hours to prepare for an exam, don't spend them all the night
before. Instead, study for one hour a day over five days. You'll feel less stressed, and you'll remember much
more when it counts.

Listen to a talk in an economics class, then answer questions 13–16.
Professor: Today I want to introduce a concept from economics called opportunity cost. Whenever you make a
choice, opportunity cost is the value of the next-best option that you give up. For example, if you spend
Saturday afternoon studying, the opportunity cost might be the film you would otherwise have watched, or the
money you could have earned at a part-time job.
Opportunity cost is useful because it reminds us that resources—time, money, energy—are limited. Every
decision to use them one way is also a decision not to use them another way. A government that spends
heavily on building new roads, for instance, has less money available for schools or hospitals.
Economists argue that good decisions are those where the benefit gained is greater than the opportunity cost. It
sounds simple, but people often ignore it, focusing only on what they gain and forgetting what they give up. In
the next class, I'll show you how businesses use this idea when deciding what to produce.

Take an Interview
An interviewer will ask you questions. Answer and be sure to say as much as you can in the time allowed. No preparation
time is provided.
You have agreed to take part in a research study about technology and everyday life. You will have a short
online interview with a researcher. The researcher will ask you some questions.
Interviewer: Thank you for joining me today. [2-second pause] First, could you tell me how often you use a
smartphone on a typical day?
Interviewer: Thanks. Some people feel that technology brings people closer together, while others believe it
makes us more isolated. What is your view, and why do you think so?
Interviewer: Next, I'd like your opinion on this. Some people say that children today spend too much time in
front of screens. Do you agree? Why or why not?
Interviewer: Finally, do you think that schools or governments should teach people how to use technology
responsibly? Why or why not?
